SEC 7 · LEC 1 · TEACH

Light in, electricity out

Simple story first, then the official names, then how it actually works with numbers you can check.
🧒 Like you're 5
Sunlight is a rain of tiny packets of energy (the photons from Section 5). A solar cell is a sandwich made of two slightly different slices of crystal. Where the slices touch there is a built-in one-way slope. A packet of light knocks an electron loose, like a ball knocked off a shelf. The slope rolls it to one side, and the only way back is the long way round, through a wire, and through your equipment on the way. That flow is electricity. Nothing moves, nothing burns.
Two catches. First, each crystal only uses packets of the right size: too small and they pass straight through, too big and the extra is wasted as heat. So the best cells stack three crystals, each tuned to a different colour, and catch about 30% of the light. Second, sunlight spreads out exactly like the radio signal did: twice as far from the Sun, a quarter as much. At Jupiter it is 27 times weaker than here, so the Juno probe needs three panels, each as long as a bus, to make what a hair dryer uses.

🏷 The official names (name first, then what it means)

  • Solar array (solar panel, photovoltaic array): many solar cells (photovoltaic cells, PV cells) wired together.
  • Solar flux (solar irradiance): sunlight power per square metre. At Earth's distance it is the solar constant, 1,361 W/m². Astronomical unit, AU: the Earth–Sun distance, 150 million km. Inverse-square law.
  • Deployable array (solar wings) and body-mounted array. A solar array drive turns the wings to face the Sun. Hold-down and release mechanism: what lets the folded wings go, often a small pyrotechnic cutter.
  • Photovoltaic effect: light producing a voltage in a material.
  • Semiconductor: a material such as silicon that conducts a little. Doping: adding a trace of another element. n-type: extra electrons. p-type: missing electrons, called holes.
  • p–n junction: where the two meet. Its built-in electric field (in the depletion region) is the one-way slope.
  • Band gap: the energy a photon needs to free an electron in that material.
  • Multi-junction cell (triple-junction, 3J): several junctions stacked. Usual stack: gallium indium phosphide / gallium arsenide / germanium.
  • Efficiency: electrical power out ÷ sunlight power in.
  • I–V curve: current against voltage for a cell. Maximum power point: the spot on it where volts × amps is largest.
  • Thermal cycling: repeated heating and cooling, once per orbit. Beginning of life / end of life power: arrays fade with age.

🛠 How it actually works

StepThe mechanism, with numbers
1 · FluxFlux = 1,361 ÷ (distance in AU)². Mercury (0.39): 9,100. Venus (0.72): 2,600. Mars (1.52): 590. Jupiter (5.2): 50. Saturn (9.5): 15 W/m².
2 · Two slicesSilicon with a trace of phosphorus has spare electrons (n). With a trace of boron it has gaps where electrons are missing (p). Join them: electrons near the boundary drop into gaps, leaving a thin zone with a permanent electric field.
3 · Photon inA photon with at least the band-gap energy frees an electron and leaves a hole. Silicon's gap is 1.1 eV, so any light shorter than 1,240 ÷ 1.1 ≈ 1,100 nm works (the eV rule from Section 5).
4 · SortedThe built-in field sweeps the electron to the n side and the hole to the p side. That piles up charge: about 0.6 V for silicon. Connect a wire and electrons flow from n, through the load, to p.
5 · The lossesPhotons below the gap pass through unused. Photons above it lose their extra as heat. One material can never beat about 33%; real silicon reaches about 20%.
6 · Three layersTop, gap 1.9 eV: takes light up to 650 nm. Middle, 1.4 eV: up to 890 nm. Bottom, 0.67 eV: up to 1,850 nm. Each uses its colours well and lets the rest through: about 30%, 2.7 V per cell.
7 · PowerP = flux × area × efficiency × cos(tilt). 1 m² at Earth: 1,361 × 0.30 ≈ 410 W. Tilt the panel 60° away from the Sun and you get half.
8 · HeatOn the slide's graph the current stays near 3.5 A while the top voltage falls from 0.63 V at 25 °C to 0.55 V at 60 °C. Power is volts × amps, so a hot cell gives less: about 0.2–0.4% per degree. A panel is at its best cold, just out of Earth's shadow.
9 · AgeRadiation knocks atoms out of place: roughly 1–3% of power lost per year. Arrays are sized for the last day of the mission.

🧮 The recipe (for the quiz)

1. Flux = 1,361 ÷ (distance in AU)².
2. Power per square metre = flux × efficiency × losses. Here: 30% cells, and 0.8 for wiring, gaps between cells and ageing.
3. Area = power needed ÷ power per square metre.
Example, Mars: 590 × 0.30 × 0.8 = 142 W per m². 1,000 ÷ 142 = 7.1 m².
Check on a real one: Juno carries about 60 m² of cells. Near Earth they would give about 14,000 W. At Jupiter, 27 times less sunlight: about 500 W.
If the quiz gives different numbers for efficiency or losses, use theirs. The three steps stay the same.

▲ Where the instructor was loose

  • "We multiply by three the efficiency." No. One layer gets about 20%, three get about 30%. The layers share the same sunlight; each takes the colours it uses best. It is 1.5 times better, not 3.
  • The photon gives the electron "inertia to move through the circuit". The photon only frees the electron. What pushes it one way is the electric field built into the junction. Without that field the electron would just fall back.
  • His account of which way electrons and holes go is tangled. The clean version: freed electrons collect on the n side, holes on the p side, and electrons travel through the outside wire from n to p.
  • "Low Earth orbit… around two hours… 12 cycles a day." Low orbits take 90 to 100 minutes (Section 1), so about 15 or 16 a day: nearly 6,000 hot-cold cycles a year.
  • Slide: "Thermal cycles: low efficiencies". Two different things. Being hot lowers efficiency. Cycling between hot and cold cracks joints and ages the array.
  • "I think this image is from Rosetta." It looks like the Juno array in a clean room, the same photo as in Section 2. I can't be certain.
  • "The maximum was around 40%." Flight cells are 30–32%. Laboratory records with concentrated light are about 47%.
  • He was right that no solar-powered craft has yet worked beyond Jupiter's distance, and that a cold panel fresh from the shadow gives the most power.
  • Not said: arrays make nothing in the shadow. That is the reason for the next lecture, batteries.

🗣 Say it out loud

Friend: "How do solar panels actually make electricity?"
You: A solar cell is two slices of crystal with a built-in one-way slope where they meet. A packet of light knocks an electron loose, the slope pushes it to one side, and it has to go round through the wire to get back. That flow is the current. The best space cells stack three layers for three colours and turn about 30% of the light into power. And sunlight thins out with distance squared, so at Jupiter you need panels the size of buses.
