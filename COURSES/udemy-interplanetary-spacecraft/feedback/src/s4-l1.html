SEC 4 · LEC 1 · TEACH

A rocket pushes on its own exhaust

Simple story first, then the official names, then how it actually works with numbers you can check.
🧒 Like you're 5
Sit on a skateboard holding a bowling ball. Throw the ball backward as hard as you can. You roll forward. You didn't push on the ground or the air. You pushed on the ball, and the ball pushed back on you. That is every rocket ever built: the ball is the gas it throws out the back. Throw it faster, or throw more of it each second, and you get a bigger push. That push is called thrust.
There are three kinds of thrower. Chemical is a fire hose: a huge amount of stuff, pretty fast, empty in minutes. Electric is a pea-shooter that fires each pea ten times faster than the hose: the push is tiny, about the weight of a few sheets of paper, but one small bag of peas lasts for years. Nuclear is a kettle heated by a reactor instead of a fire: it throws its steam about twice as fast as the fire hose can.

🏷 The official names (name first, then what it means)

  • Space propulsion (spacecraft propulsion, in-space propulsion): any system that changes a spacecraft's velocity.
  • Thrust (F): the force an engine makes, in newtons (N). 1 N is about the weight of an apple.
  • Newton's third law of motion (action–reaction, the reaction principle): forces come in equal and opposite pairs. The deeper rule behind it is conservation of momentum.
  • Propellant (reaction mass, working fluid): the stuff thrown out. A reaction engine is any engine that works by throwing it.
  • Chemical propulsion (chemical rocket; the instructor's "chemical internal heat engine"): the energy comes from burning the propellant itself.
  • Electric propulsion, EP (low-thrust propulsion): electricity accelerates the propellant. Main kinds: the ion thruster (gridded ion engine) and the Hall-effect thruster.
  • Nuclear thermal propulsion, NTP (nuclear thermal rocket): a fission reactor heats hydrogen. Its cousin nuclear electric propulsion, NEP uses a reactor to power electric thrusters.
  • Gravity assist (slingshot, flyby, swing-by): the no-propellant way to change velocity. Repeat from Section 3.
  • Requirements: what the mission needs the engine to do. Choosing against them is a trade study.

🛠 How it actually works

StepThe mechanism, with numbers
1 · Momentum is keptMomentum = mass × velocity, and the total can't change. A 1,000 kg ship at rest throws 1 kg backward at 3,000 m/s. 1 × 3,000 = 999 × v, so the ship moves forward at 3 m/s. Air appears nowhere in that sum.
2 · ThrustThrust is how fast you throw momentum away: F = ṁ × vₑ. Throw 0.1 kg every second at 3,000 m/s and F = 300 N.
3 · Δva = F ÷ m = 300 ÷ 1,000 = 0.3 m/s². Burn 10 s: Δv = 3 m/s, using 1 kg. Same answer as step 1. Thrust and time build the Δv your orbit change needs.
4 · Why electric is weakSame 300 N from 0.01 kg/s at 30,000 m/s uses ten times less propellant. But the jet's power is ½ṁvₑ²: 0.45 MW for the first, 4.5 MW for the second. A chemical engine gets that from the fire. An electric one needs solar panels, and the whole ISS makes about 0.1–0.2 MW. So real electric thrusters run at a few kW and push with a fraction of a newton.
5 · Real electricNASA's NEXT ion thruster (the slide photo): 6.9 kW in, 0.236 N out (the weight of 5 sheets of paper), exhaust at 41 km/s, Isp 4,190 s. The Dawn probe took 4 days to go 0–100 km/h, then thrust for 5.9 years: total Δv 11.5 km/s from 425 kg of xenon.
6 · Why nuclear wins on speedExhaust speed goes as √(temperature ÷ molecule mass). Burning hydrogen and oxygen makes steam, mass 18. A reactor can heat plain hydrogen, mass 2, to about the same 2,700 K. Lighter molecules fly faster: Isp ≈ 900 s against 450 s.
7 · What mileage buysFor a 5 km/s Δv the rocket equation says: Isp 320 s needs 80% of the ship to be propellant; Isp 3,000 s needs 16%.

▲ Where the instructor was loose

  • "We need propulsion to increase velocity." Half of it. You also need it to slow down (braking into orbit, landing) and to change direction. Δv is a change in velocity, any direction.
  • "Electric propulsion… I think it was already implemented in the 1980s… kind of new." Wrong by two decades. Ion engines were first fired in space in 1964 (NASA's SERT-1; the Soviet Zond 2 used plasma thrusters the same year). Soviet weather satellites used Hall thrusters from 1971. Deep Space 1 (1998) was the first probe to cruise on an ion engine. Today most new communications satellites and every Starlink carry electric thrusters. It is mainstream.
  • "Nuclear… being developed especially by NASA right now." The US built and ground-tested nuclear rockets (NERVA) until 1972. The recent NASA and DARPA flight demo, DRACO, was cancelled in 2025 as far as I know. No nuclear rocket has ever flown.
  • Not said: an RTG is not nuclear propulsion. Voyager and New Horizons carry plutonium power packs (radioisotope thermoelectric generators). Those make electricity. They don't push anything.
  • Not said: the real sorting rule. The three families differ in where the energy comes from: inside the propellant (chemical), from solar panels (electric), from a reactor (nuclear). That one idea explains the whole table above.
  • Not said: there are engines with no propellant at all. A solar sail is pushed by sunlight. Rare, but it exists.
  • "Chemical internal heat engines" is his own phrase. The term you will meet everywhere else is chemical propulsion or chemical rocket.
SEC 4 · LEC 1 · VOCABULARY

Every word used, in plain English

40 entries: the lecture's words plus every term I used on the other pages. "Repeat" = you have met it before. Names marked "Add to Who's Who" are missions or programs.
Space propulsion — any system that changes a spacecraft's velocity. The spacecraft's legs.
Velocity — speed plus direction. Changing either one counts as a velocity change. Repeat.
Delta-v (Δv) — the size of a velocity change; the currency every maneuver is priced in. Repeat.
Orbit change / maneuver — a planned burn (or flyby) that moves you from one orbit to another.
Gravitational field — the region around a body where its gravity pulls on you. Repeat.
Slingshot = gravity assist — swinging past a moving planet to change your speed and direction without propellant. Repeat, scene 7.
Thrust (F) — the force an engine produces, in newtons. The push.
Newton (N) — the unit of force. About the weight of one apple. 1 kN = 1,000 N, 1 MN = a million.
Accelerate / acceleration — to change velocity / how fast velocity changes (m/s²). a = F ÷ m.
Newton's third law — every force has an equal and opposite partner. Push the gas back, the gas pushes you forward. Skateboard and bowling ball.
Action / reaction — the two halves of that pair: gas thrown back / ship pushed forward.
Momentum — mass × velocity. The total never changes unless something outside pushes. The bookkeeping behind thrust.
Eject / expel — throw out. A rocket ejects propellant.
Propellant = reaction mass — the material thrown out to make thrust. Not the same word as fuel: fuel is only the part that burns.
Reaction engine — any engine that works by throwing mass backward: rockets, ion thrusters, jets.
Exhaust — the propellant after it has left the engine. Its speed is the exhaust velocity, vₑ.
Mass flow rate (ṁ, 'm-dot') — kilograms of propellant thrown out each second.
Chemical propulsion — engines whose energy comes from burning propellant. The instructor's 'chemical internal heat engines'. Fire hose.
Heat engine — any machine that turns heat into motion. A rocket nozzle does exactly that.
Rocket — a reaction engine that carries everything it throws, including its own oxygen.
Liquid propellant — propellant stored as liquid in tanks, as opposed to a solid block. Next lecture.
Electric propulsion (EP) — engines that use electricity to accelerate propellant. Very fast exhaust, very small thrust. Pea-shooter.
Low-thrust propulsion — the family name for engines that push gently for a very long time. Mostly means electric.
Ion / ionize — an atom with an electron knocked off, so electricity can grab and fling it / to make ions.
Ion thruster (gridded ion engine) — ionizes xenon gas and fires the ions out through charged metal grids at 30–40 km/s. The blue glow in the slide.
Hall-effect thruster — the other common electric engine; uses a magnetic field instead of grids. More thrust, a bit less Isp.
Xenon — a heavy, non-reactive gas. The usual propellant for ion engines because it is easy to ionize and store.
Nuclear propulsion — using a fission reactor as the energy source for an engine.
Nuclear thermal propulsion (NTP) — reactor heats hydrogen, hydrogen shoots out a nozzle. Isp about 900 s. Reactor-heated kettle.
Nuclear electric propulsion (NEP) — reactor makes electricity, electricity runs ion or Hall thrusters.
Fission / reactor — splitting heavy atoms to release heat / the machine that does it under control.
RTG — radioisotope thermoelectric generator: a plutonium power pack that makes electricity. Not an engine. Voyager, New Horizons.
Specific impulse (Isp) — engine efficiency in seconds: how much push you get per kilogram of propellant. Miles per gallon. Full treatment next lecture.
Requirements — what the mission needs: how much Δv, how fast, how heavy, how much power.
Trade-off / trade study — giving up one good thing to get another / the formal comparison of options. Thrust against mileage.
Controversial / political — here: people worry about launching a reactor, so nuclear engines need government approval.
NEXT — NASA's Evolutionary Xenon Thruster, the ion engine in the slide photo. A version flew on DART in 2021. Add to Who's Who.
Dawn / Deep Space 1 / DART — NASA probes that flew on ion engines: asteroid belt (2007), technology test (1998), asteroid impact test (2021). Add to Who's Who.
NERVA / DRACO — US nuclear rocket programs: NERVA ground-tested until 1972; DRACO, a planned flight demo, cancelled in 2025. Add to Who's Who.
SERT-1 / Zond 2 — the first electric thrusters fired in space, both in 1964 (US / Soviet). Add to Who's Who.

Last page: prove it by talking

After the spread and the 3-line summary, with everything closed. Send me what you said and I will grade it like a friend would.

🗣 The friend's opening question

"There's no air in space. So what does a rocket even push against?"

✅ Three sentences you should be able to say

  • It pushes against its own exhaust. That's Newton's third law: throw mass out the back and you get shoved forward, air or no air.
  • The push is called thrust, and it is how much stuff you throw each second times how fast you throw it.
  • There are three kinds of engine. Chemical rockets push hard but burn through propellant fast, electric engines push very gently but sip it for years, and nuclear sits in between and has never flown.

❓ Follow-ups a friend might ask

  • "If electric is so efficient, why not use it for everything?" (the push is a few sheets of paper; you can't launch or brake into orbit with that)
  • "Why is it so weak?" (fast exhaust takes huge power, and solar panels only give kilowatts)
  • "Is the nuclear stuff on Voyager an engine?" (no, an RTG makes electricity)
  • "Can you change orbit without an engine?" (yes, a gravity assist, but only when a planet is in the right place)
  • "How do you choose?" (the mission's requirements: how much Δv and how fast you need it)
