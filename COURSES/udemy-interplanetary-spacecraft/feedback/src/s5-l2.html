SEC 5 · LEC 2 · TEACH

Hard hitters get stopped. Radio slips through.

Simple story first, then the official names, then how it actually works with numbers you can check.
🧒 Like you're 5
The air around the Earth is a wall with two windows in it. One window lets in ordinary light, the kind your eyes use. The other lets in radio. Everything else gets stopped. The dangerous rays (ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays) are soaked up high in the sky, which is what keeps us alive. Heat rays (infrared) are soaked up by the water and carbon dioxide in the air. And very long radio waves bounce off an electric layer near the top, like a ball off a ceiling.
Why? Light of every kind arrives in tiny packets, and the faster it wiggles, the harder each packet hits. Hard-hitting packets smash into air atoms and are used up. Gentle radio packets slip between them untouched. So if you want to talk to a spacecraft from the ground you must use the radio window. And if you want to see X-rays or infrared from the stars, you have to lift the telescope above the wall. That is why Hubble and Webb are in space.

🏷 The official names (name first, then what it means)

  • Atmospheric opacity (its opposite is transmittance): the share of radiation the air stops, as a function of wavelength.
  • Atmospheric window: a wavelength range the air lets through. The optical window (visible light) and the radio window (about 1 cm to 10 m, or 30 GHz down to 30 MHz).
  • Photon: one packet of electromagnetic energy. Photon energy E = h × f, where h is the Planck constant.
  • Absorption: a photon giving its energy to an atom or molecule and vanishing. Ionization when it knocks an electron off; molecular vibration when it sets a molecule shaking.
  • Ionospheric reflection: long radio waves being turned back by the ionosphere's free electrons. The cut-off is the critical frequency, around 10 MHz.
  • Radio-frequency bands (ITU bands): VLF, LF, MF, HF, VHF, UHF, SHF, EHF. Each covers ten times the frequency of the one before. Microwaves are roughly 1–100 GHz.
  • Letter bands (IEEE radar bands): L, S, C, X, Ku, K, Ka. Wartime radar code letters, deliberately meaningless. Ku and Ka mean "K-under" and "K-above".
  • Uplink: Earth to spacecraft. Downlink: spacecraft to Earth.
  • Deep space, in radio rules: farther than 2 million km from Earth. Its bands are protected because the signals are so faint.
  • ITU, the International Telecommunication Union: the UN body that decides who may use which frequency. Frequency allocation.
  • Rain fade: rain and water vapour weakening signals above about 10 GHz.

🛠 How it actually works

StepThe mechanism, with numbers
1 · Photon energyE = h × f. Handy form: energy in eV = 1,240 ÷ wavelength in nanometres. UV at 250 nm: 5 eV. Green light, 550 nm: 2.3 eV. Infrared, 10,000 nm: 0.12 eV. X-band radio, 3.6 cm: 0.000035 eV.
2 · Gamma, X, UVSplitting an oxygen molecule takes about 5 eV; tearing an electron off nitrogen takes 15. These photons have that, so they are used up 50–100 km overhead. That is what makes the ozone layer and the ionosphere (Section 2).
3 · Visible1.8 to 3.1 eV: not enough to break air molecules, far too much to match their vibrations. Nothing in clean air can take it, so it passes.
4 · InfraredAbout 0.1 eV is exactly what sets water and CO₂ molecules bending and stretching. Absorbed. This is the greenhouse effect, and why infrared telescopes fly.
5 · Radio windowMillionths of an eV: too little to excite anything. Transparent from about 30 MHz to 30 GHz. Every spacecraft link lives here. Edges: water vapour absorbs near 22 GHz, oxygen near 60 GHz, and rain hurts above 10 GHz.
6 · Long radioBelow a critical frequency ≈ 9 × √(electrons per m³) hertz, the ionosphere's electrons move fast enough to send the wave back. With 10¹² electrons per m³ that is 9 MHz. So shortwave radio skips round the world, and can't reach a spacecraft.
7 · Up ≠ downDeep-space X-band: uplink 7.2 GHz, downlink 8.4 GHz. If both were the same, the craft's 20 W transmitter would deafen its own receiver, which is listening for a signal a billion billion times weaker.
8 · Why go higherSame dish, Ka (32 GHz) instead of X (8.4): the wavelength is 3.8 times shorter, so the gain is 3.8² ≈ 14 times higher. Price: rain fade and a beam 3.8 times narrower to point.

▲ Where the instructor was loose

  • "High-energy radiation interacts with any particle, basically always." Not quite. A photon is absorbed when its energy matches something a molecule can do with it. Gamma, X and UV have enough to rip atoms apart. Infrared matches molecular vibrations. Visible and radio match nothing, so they pass. It is about matching, not just "more energy".
  • "Long-wavelength radio waves blocked." By a different mechanism from the rest: the ionosphere reflects them. That is why shortwave radio reaches round the planet.
  • Not said: why uplink and downlink differ. So the spacecraft can transmit and listen at once without deafening itself.
  • Not said: why anyone wants the higher bands. The same dish gives more gain and there is more room for data. The cost is weather and tighter pointing.
  • Not said: who hands out frequencies. The ITU, by international treaty.
  • The slide's table lumps Ku, K and Ka together as "K, 12–40 GHz", and gives X-band's short end as 2.4 cm where 2.5 cm is correct.
  • He was right that it is no accident we see in visible light: it is the window where the Sun is brightest and the air is clear.

🗣 The friend's opening question

"Why do they put telescopes in space? And why does everything in space use radio?"

✅ Three sentences you should be able to say

  • Because the air is a wall with two windows. Ordinary light gets through one and radio gets through the other, and nearly everything else is stopped.
  • It comes down to how hard each packet of light hits. X-rays and ultraviolet hit hard enough to break air molecules, so they are absorbed high up, and infrared gets soaked up by water and CO₂. Radio is too gentle to do anything, so it sails through.
  • So telescopes for X-rays or infrared have to sit above the air, and anything you want to say to a spacecraft has to go through the radio window, in agreed bands like S, X and Ka.

❓ Follow-ups a friend might ask

  • "Why can we see, then?" (our eyes use exactly the window where the air is clear and the Sun is brightest)
  • "Does all radio get through?" (no: very long waves bounce off the ionosphere, and rain hurts the highest bands)
  • "What do the band letters mean?" (nothing: old radar code names; X is around 8 GHz)
  • "Is Wi-Fi one of those?" (yes: 2.4 GHz is S-band, 5 GHz is C-band)
  • "What's uplink and downlink?" (ground to spacecraft, spacecraft to ground, on different frequencies so they don't clash)
