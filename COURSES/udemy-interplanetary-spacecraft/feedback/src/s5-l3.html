SEC 5 · LEC 3 · TEACH

Shouting across a stadium

Simple story first, then the official names, then how it actually works with numbers you can check.
🧒 Like you're 5
You want to tell a friend a secret across a huge, noisy stadium. First you pack the message small and add a few spare words so a missed word can be guessed (encode). You put it on a whistle note that carries (modulate), move that note up to the pitch you were told to use (up-convert), and blow as hard as you can (high-power amplifier) through a megaphone (the antenna).
On the way, your shout does not get eaten. It spreads out. Twice as far away, it covers four times the area, so each ear gets a quarter of it. A helper halfway hears you faintly and shouts it on again (the repeater). Your friend cups a big ear (a dish) and uses a hearing aid that adds almost no hiss of its own (the low-noise amplifier), then undoes every step in reverse. Whether the secret arrives depends on one thing only: how loud your shout is compared with the crowd noise at your friend's ear. That comparison is the whole link equation.

🏷 The official names (name first, then what it means)

  • Communications link: one complete radio path, transmitter to receiver. Uplink, downlink. The space segment is the satellite; the ground segment is the Earth stations.
  • Source coding (compression) and channel coding (forward error correction, FEC): the two jobs the lecture's "encoder" does. One removes waste; the other adds protective spare bits. Codec = coder + decoder.
  • Modem: modulator + demodulator.
  • Up-converter / down-converter (frequency converter, mixer): shifts a modulated signal to a different frequency without changing what it carries. The low starting frequency is the intermediate frequency, IF; the on-air one is the radio frequency, RF.
  • High-power amplifier, HPA (power amplifier): the last stage before the transmit antenna.
  • Free-space path loss, Lₛ (spreading loss; the slide's "free space loss"). Atmospheric loss, Lₐ (atmospheric attenuation).
  • Transponder (the lecture's "satellite repeater"): receives, shifts frequency, amplifies, retransmits. A satellite that only does this is a bent-pipe relay.
  • Low-noise amplifier, LNA: the first stage after the receive antenna.
  • Thermal noise; noise power density, N₀ = k × T. Boltzmann constant, k. System noise temperature, T.
  • EIRP, effective isotropic radiated power: P × Gₜ. G/T ("G over T"): receive gain ÷ noise temperature, a station's figure of merit.
  • Signal-to-noise ratio; here S/N₀. Link budget: the gains-and-losses ledger. Decibel, dB. Link margin.

🛠 How it actually works

StepThe mechanism, with numbers
1 · EncodeSqueeze out the waste (compression), then add structured spare bits so the far end can repair errors. Good codes let you send about 5 times more data over the same link.
2 · Modulate, then moveWrite the bits onto a carrier at an easy fixed frequency, say 70 MHz. Then mix it with a 7,100 MHz tone: out comes the same signal at 7,170 MHz, in the X-band uplink slot. Two steps, because precise modulators are easy to build low and one design then serves any band.
3 · Amplify outThe signal is strong and clean here, so noise is no worry. Only watts matter: 20 W on Cassini, 20,000 W on a Deep Space Network dish.
4 · Space lossNothing absorbs the wave. It spreads over a sphere of area 4πd². Twice the distance, a quarter of the power. In the standard form Lₛ = (4πd ÷ λ)². To a GEO satellite: 10²⁰. Saturn to Earth: 2.5 × 10²⁹.
5 · RepeatA relay satellite hears perhaps 10⁻¹⁰ W, moves it to the downlink frequency so it cannot jam its own receiver, amplifies it about 10¹¹ times and sends it down.
6 · Amplify inEverything warm hisses with radio noise: N₀ = k × T watts in every hertz, with k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³. Whatever noise the first amplifier adds is amplified by all the stages after it, so that one stage must be the quietest thing in the station. NASA chills its to about 15 K. Then down-convert, demodulate, decode.
7 · The ratioAn amplifier cannot tell signal from noise; it lifts both. So the ratio fixed at the first amplifier is all you ever get. Data rate ≈ S/N₀ ÷ (a factor of about 2 to 4 that the code needs).

Reading the answer. The signal is as strong as the noise in a 400,000 Hz slice. Divide by about 4 for the code's needs and a safety margin: roughly 100,000 bits per second. Cassini's real top rate was 166,000.
Why rates collapse with distance. Voyager 1 is 17 times farther than Saturn, so its space loss is 17² ≈ 300 times worse. It sends 160 bits per second. New Horizons needed 15 months to return its Pluto data.

📐 Decibels in one minute

A decibel counts multiplications. +3 dB = ×2 · +10 dB = ×10 · +20 dB = ×100 · −30 dB = ÷1,000. Multiplying numbers means adding their decibels, which is why the right-hand column simply adds up: 13 + 47 − 294 − 0.2 + 74 = −160.
dBW means "compared with 1 watt". dBm means "compared with 1 milliwatt". You have seen this already: a Wi-Fi router sends about +20 dBm (0.1 W), your laptop reports the signal as about −60 dBm, and its noise floor is near −95 dBm. That is a link budget. Cassini's is the same sum with bigger numbers.

🎚 The only knobs there are

  • More power (P): costs solar panels and heat.
  • Bigger or higher-frequency dish (Gₜ): costs mass and pointing accuracy.
  • Bigger ground dish, or several combined (Gᵣ): 70 m is about the limit.
  • Colder receiver (T): halve the noise temperature, double the data.
  • Better codes: free decibels from mathematics.
  • Distance (Lₛ): the one you cannot touch, and by far the largest term.
A decibel gained anywhere is worth the same as a decibel gained anywhere else.

▲ Where the instructor was loose

  • "1/k… is just a relation for wavelength and other parameters." Wrong. k is the Boltzmann constant, 1.38 × 10⁻²³ joules per kelvin. It turns the receiver's temperature into noise power: N₀ = k × T. It has nothing to do with wavelength.
  • The low-noise amplifier must "avoid mistaking the signal for noise". No amplifier can tell them apart. It lifts both. The point is that it adds very little noise of its own, because anything the first stage adds is magnified by every stage after it.
  • "High-power amplifiers know the signal, so it's safe and they can amplify more." The real reason is simpler: at the transmitter the signal is enormous compared with any noise, so only output power matters.
  • "Free-space losses." Right that they grow with distance. Not said: nothing is lost to anything. The wave spreads, and the receiver catches a smaller share.
  • "Encoders… convert my voice into an electrical signal." That is a microphone. Encoding means compressing the data and adding error-correcting bits. The second job is what makes deep-space links work at all.
  • "Up-conversion is similar to modulation." Different jobs. Modulation writes the message on a carrier. Up-conversion moves the finished signal to another frequency and changes nothing in it.
  • "Gain is related to the high power and the directivity." Antenna gain is directivity only. Power is its own term.
  • His diagram is a relay link: ground to satellite to ground. That is a communications satellite, the "relay" type from Section 1. A science probe is one end of the link, not the middle.
  • Not said: what the ratio buys. Bits per second. That is the reason anyone computes it.
SEC 5 · LEC 3 · VOCABULARY

Every word used, in plain English

40 entries. "Repeat" = you have met it before.
Link — one radio connection from a transmitter to a receiver.
Component — one part of a system.
Modulator / demodulator (modem) — writes the signal onto a carrier / takes it off again. Repeat, L1.
Up-converter / down-converter — moves a signal to a higher / lower frequency without changing what it carries. Changing lanes, not changing the cargo.
Encoder / decoder (codec) — turns information into a compact, protected code / turns it back.
Format / code — an agreed way of writing information as symbols.
Compressor / compression — squeezing data into fewer bits by removing what is repeated or unneeded.
Error-correcting code — spare bits added in a pattern so the receiver can find and fix mistakes.
Analog signal — a smoothly varying signal, like a voice.
Electric pulses / digital signal — information as a string of 1s and 0s.
Bit / bits per second — one 1-or-0 / how many are sent each second: the data rate.
Satellite repeater = transponder — equipment on a satellite that receives a signal, boosts it and sends it on. The helper halfway across the stadium.
Attenuation — weakening.
Atmospheric attenuation = atmospheric loss (Lₐ) — the weakening caused by the air and rain. Repeat, L2.
Free-space loss (Lₛ) — the weakening caused by the wave spreading out over distance. Twice as far, a quarter as strong.
Amplifier / amplify — a device that makes a signal stronger / to do so.
High-power amplifier (HPA) — the amplifier that feeds the transmitting antenna. Blowing hard.
Low-noise amplifier (LNA) — the first amplifier after the receiving antenna, built to add almost no noise. A hearing aid with no hiss.
Noise — random electrical hiss that is always present and hides weak signals.
Thermal noise — noise caused by heat: every warm object and circuit makes it.
Signal (S) — the power of the wanted signal at the receiver.
N₀ (N-zero) — noise power in each hertz of bandwidth. N₀ = k × T.
Signal-to-noise ratio (S/N₀) — how strong the signal is compared with the noise. Sets the data rate.
Boltzmann constant (k) — 1.38 × 10⁻²³ joules per kelvin. Converts temperature into noise power.
Kelvin (K) — temperature counted from absolute zero. 20 K is −253 °C. Repeat.
System temperature (T) — a single number for all the noise in a receiving station, as if it came from an object that warm.
Transmitted power (P) — watts fed to the transmitting antenna.
Gain (Gₜ, Gᵣ) — how much the transmit / receive antenna concentrates the signal. Repeat, L1.
EIRP — power × transmit gain: how strong the transmitter looks from far away.
G/T — receive gain ÷ noise temperature: how good a listening station is.
Link budget — the list of every gain and loss between transmitter and receiver, added up. A bank statement for the signal.
Decibel (dB) / dBW / dBm — a scale that counts multiplications / relative to 1 watt / to 1 milliwatt. Repeat, L1.
Margin — the safety allowance left over after the sum.
Transmitting / receiving Earth station — the ground end that sends / listens.
Space segment / ground segment — the satellite part / the Earth part of a system.
Uplink / downlink — ground to satellite / satellite to ground. Repeat, L2.
Input / output information — what goes into the chain / what comes out the far end.
Distorted — changed in shape on the way.
Relay / bent pipe — a satellite that only passes signals on. Repeat, Section 1.
Voyager 1 / New Horizons / Deep Space Network — the farthest probe / the Pluto probe / NASA's 70 m dishes. Who's Who.

🗣 The friend's opening question

"Why does it take so long to get pictures back from far-away probes? Radio moves at the speed of light."

✅ Three sentences you should be able to say

  • The radio is fast, but the signal arrives unbelievably faint. Nothing absorbs it; it just spreads out, so twice as far away you catch a quarter as much.
  • At the receiver it has to be picked out of the electrical hiss that everything warm makes. What matters is the signal compared with that noise, and that ratio sets how many bits per second you can send.
  • So engineers add up a budget: transmitter power, both dishes, the distance, the receiver's noise. From Saturn that gives about a hundred thousand bits a second. From Voyager it is 160.

❓ Follow-ups a friend might ask

  • "Can't they just amplify it more?" (an amplifier boosts the noise by the same amount; the ratio doesn't change)
  • "So how do you improve it?" (more power, bigger dishes, a colder receiver, cleverer codes)
  • "What's a low-noise amplifier?" (the first amplifier at the receiver, chilled so it adds almost no hiss of its own)
  • "What does a relay satellite do?" (hears the faint uplink, moves it to another frequency, boosts it and sends it back down)
  • "What are decibels for?" (they turn all those multiplications into one column you can add)
