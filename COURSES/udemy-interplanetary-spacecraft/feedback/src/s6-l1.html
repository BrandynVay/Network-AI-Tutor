SEC 6 · LEC 1 · TEACH

A thermostat for pointing

Simple story first, then the official names, then how it actually works with numbers you can check.
🧒 Like you're 5
A spacecraft has two different problems: where it is (its orbit) and which way it faces (its attitude). The dish has to face Earth and the panels have to face the Sun, and nothing in space holds it steady. So it works like the thermostat in your house. A thermostat feels the temperature, compares it with what you asked for and switches the heater. The spacecraft looks at the stars to see which way it is facing, compares that with where it should face, and nudges itself with a small twist. Then it looks again. Forever. That loop is the whole system.
The second half is about spinning. Toss a coin spinning flat like a frisbee and it stays steady. Toss a pencil spinning around its length and it soon starts to wobble and ends up tumbling end over end. Things like to spin the "fat" way, with their weight as far from the spin line as possible. The first American satellite was a pencil spun around its length. Within hours it was tumbling. The first Soviet one was a ball, which has no fat way, so nothing could go wrong.

🏷 The official names (name first, then what it means)

  • Attitude: the orientation of a spacecraft relative to a reference frame. ADCS, attitude determination and control system (also AOCS when orbit control is included).
  • Roll, pitch, yaw: rotations about the three body axes. Together they are a set of Euler angles.
  • Reference frame: what you measure against. An inertial frame is fixed to the distant stars. The orbital frame (LVLH, local vertical / local horizontal) turns with the orbit: one axis to the planet, one along the track, one across. The slide's picture is the orbital frame. Nadir: straight down to the planet.
  • Attitude determination (measuring) and attitude control (correcting). Attitude error: measured minus wanted.
  • Closed-loop control (feedback control). The rule the computer uses is the control law.
  • Sensor and actuator: the parts that measure and the parts that push. Details next lecture.
  • Torque (moment): force × lever arm, in newton-metres. The turning version of force.
  • Pointing accuracy (how close to the target) and pointing stability (how steady). Jitter: fast pointing vibration. Arcsecond: 1/3600 of a degree.
  • Moment of inertia, I: the turning version of mass. Principal axes: the major axis (maximum I), minor axis (minimum), and the intermediate axis.
  • Angular momentum, L = I × spin rate. Conserved when no torque acts.
  • Torque-free motion. The major-axis rule. Flat spin: tumbling about the major axis. Spin stabilization versus three-axis stabilization.

🛠 How it actually works

StepThe mechanism, with numbers
1 · TorqueTorque = force × lever arm. Angular speed-up = torque ÷ moment of inertia, the twin of a = F ÷ m.
2 · One turn of the loopA spacecraft with I = 100 kg·m² is 1° off target. Its wheel can twist with 0.05 N·m. Speed-up = 0.05 ÷ 100 = 0.0005 rad/s². Twist one way for 6 s, the other way for 6 s: 1° corrected in about 12 seconds. Measure again.
3 · JitterWheels are never perfectly balanced, pumps hum, panels creak as they warm. The result is a fast tremble on top of the slow correction. Hubble holds 0.007 arcseconds: the width of a hair seen from 3 km.
4 · Moment of inertiaI = sum of (mass × distance from the axis²). A 14 kg pencil 2 m long and 16 cm wide: about its long axis ½MR² = 0.044; about an axis across its middle 4.9 kg·m². 110 times bigger, because the ends are a metre from that axis.
5 · What is keptWith no outside torque, angular momentum L = I × spin rate cannot change. The spin energy is L² ÷ (2 × I): for the same L, a bigger I means less energy.
6 · The leakReal spacecraft are not rigid. Antennas flex, fuel sloshes, and each wobble turns a little spin energy into heat. L stays, energy falls, so the body slides toward the lowest-energy spin: about the biggest I.
7 · Explorer 1Spun at 750 turns a minute about its long axis: 135 joules. Its four whip antennas flexed. After the slide: 750 ÷ 110 ≈ 7 turns a minute, end over end, with about 1 joule. The other 99% went into bending wire.
8 · The fixMake the spin axis the fat one: build spinning satellites as squat drums. Or don't spin at all and run the loop on three axes.
SEC 6 · LEC 1 · FACT CHECK

Right conclusions, shaky reasons

Where the instructor was wrong or loose, the caption errors, and the question a friend would ask.

⛔ Wrong, and worth getting right

  • "Moment of inertia is the amount of mass in one direction." It is about how far the mass is from the spin axis. Along a pencil's length the mass hugs the axis, so that moment is small. Across the middle the ends are far away, so it is large. His conclusions for all three shapes are right; the reason he gives is not.
  • "Explorer 1 was completely unstable… difficult to recover data." It did tumble, and nobody expected it. But it kept working for four months and made the first great discovery of the space age: the radiation belts around the Earth.
  • "The Russians understood this… the US goes for marketing style." Nobody understood it yet. Explorer 1's tumble is how engineers learned the rule. Its pencil shape came from the rocket: the satellite was the top stage's casing with instruments in the nose.
  • "The sphere would move completely random." A sphere keeps whatever spin it starts with. It has no preferred axis, so it is neither stable nor unstable. Sputnik 1 did not care because its four antennas radiated in every direction, like the omnidirectional antennas in Section 5.

▲ Looser points

  • The rule needs a leak. For a perfectly rigid body, spin about the smallest axis is stable too. Only the in-between axis is unstable. Try it: flip your phone over a bed about each of its three axes. One of them always wobbles.
  • "The sensors determine the difference." Sensors only measure. The computer compares with the target.
  • Jitter "cannot really be corrected". It can be reduced a great deal with balanced wheels and vibration isolators. It can't be made zero.
  • "An inertial reference frame." The slide's axes (to the planet, along the velocity) turn with the orbit. That is the orbital frame. Both are used.

🗣 Say it out loud

Friend: "How does a spacecraft know which way it's facing, and how does it turn with nothing to push on?"
You: It runs a loop, like a thermostat. Sensors look at the stars or the Sun to measure which way it points. The computer compares that with where it should point. Then it twists itself a little, usually by spinning a wheel inside, and checks again. It never stops, and the pointing always trembles a tiny bit.
Friend: "Why not just spin it like a top?"
You: You can, but only the fat way. Things end up spinning with their weight as far from the axis as possible. America's first satellite was a pencil spun lengthwise and it was tumbling within hours.
SEC 6 · LEC 1 · VOCABULARY

Every word used, in plain English

40 entries. "Repeat" = you have met it before.
Attitude — which way a spacecraft is pointing. Not its height: that is altitude. Repeat.
Orientation — the direction something faces.
ADCS — attitude determination and control system: the parts that measure pointing and correct it.
Axis / axes — an imaginary line something turns around. Three of them, at right angles.
Roll / pitch / yaw — turning about the direction of travel / about the across-orbit axis / about the line to the planet. Wings tilt / nose up-down / nose left-right.
Central body — the planet being orbited. Repeat.
Velocity direction — the way the spacecraft is travelling.
Angle of attack — aircraft term: how far the nose points above the airflow. A pitch angle.
Reference frame / inertial frame — the set of directions you measure against / one fixed to the distant stars.
Attitude determination — measuring the orientation.
Attitude control — turning the spacecraft back to the wanted orientation.
Closed feedback loop — a system that measures its own result and keeps correcting it. A thermostat.
Dynamics — how something moves under the pushes and twists on it.
Sensor — a device that measures: a star camera, a Sun detector, a spin meter. Repeat; next lecture.
On-board computer — the spacecraft's processor. Compares measured with wanted.
Attitude error — the difference between where it points and where it should.
Command — an instruction sent to a device.
Actuator — a device that pushes or twists: a wheel, a thruster, a magnetic coil. Next lecture.
Torque — a twisting push: force × lever arm. Captions write 'talk'. A wrench on a bolt.
Jitter — fast, small trembling of the pointing direction.
Phenomenon / phenomena — something that happens and can be observed.
Imprecision — not being exact.
Requirement / constraint — what the mission needs / a limit that must not be broken. Repeat.
Torque-free — with no outside twist acting.
Rotational dynamics — the physics of spinning things.
Spin — to turn about an axis through yourself.
Stable / unstable — returns after a nudge / runs away after a nudge.
Moment of inertia — how hard a body is to spin about a given axis. Depends on how far its mass is from that axis. The turning version of mass.
Maximum moment of inertia axis — the axis the mass is, on average, farthest from. The 'fat way' to spin.
Homogeneous — the same all the way through. Repeat.
Rigid — does not bend or flex.
Sphere / cylinder / disk — ball / tube or rod / flat round plate.
Center of mass — the balance point of a body.
Mass distribution — where the mass is placed inside a body.
Plane — a flat sheet. Repeat.
Angular momentum — amount of spin: moment of inertia × spin rate. Kept unless a torque acts. A skater pulling in her arms.
Flat spin — a tumble about the biggest-inertia axis.
Explorer 1 — the first US satellite, 1958. A 2 m pencil that found the radiation belts. Who's Who.
Sputnik 1 — the first satellite of all, USSR, 1957. A 58 cm ball that beeped for three weeks. Who's Who.
Radiation belts (Van Allen belts) — rings of trapped high-energy particles around the Earth. Who's Who.
