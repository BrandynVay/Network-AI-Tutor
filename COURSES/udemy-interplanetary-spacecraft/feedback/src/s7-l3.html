SEC 7 · LEC 3 · TEACH

Power for where the Sun doesn't reach

Simple story first, then the official names, then how it actually works with numbers you can check.
🧒 Like you're 5
Imagine a lump of metal that is hot to the touch and stays hot for a hundred years with nothing plugged in. Plutonium-238 is that lump. Its atoms are unstable: one by one, each spits out a tiny piece of itself, and every spit is a little kick of heat. A fresh pellet glows red by itself. Nobody starts it and nobody can stop it.
Now the trick for getting electricity. Join two different materials, heat one end and keep the other end cold, and a small voltage appears between the cold ends. So you wrap hundreds of these little pairs around the hot lump and put cooling fins on the outside. That is the whole machine: no moving parts, nothing to wear out. It works in the dark, in a dust storm, at Pluto. The catches: only about 6% of the heat becomes electricity, the plutonium is rare and radioactive so launching it is a political fight, and the power slowly fades as the lump uses itself up. Voyager has been running on three of them since 1977.

🏷 The official names (name first, then what it means)

  • Radioisotope thermoelectric generator, RTG (the slide's "thermal generator"; nuclear battery): makes electricity from the heat of radioactive decay.
  • Radioisotope (radioactive isotope): a version of an element whose atoms are unstable. Radioactive decay: an unstable nucleus changing by itself and releasing energy.
  • Alpha decay: decay by throwing out an alpha particle (α; a helium nucleus: 2 protons + 2 neutrons).
  • Half-life: the time for half the atoms to decay. Plutonium-238: 87.7 years.
  • Plutonium-238, Pu-238: the usual fuel, used as a ceramic, plutonium dioxide. It decays to uranium-234.
  • Seebeck effect (thermoelectric effect): a temperature difference across two joined materials produces a voltage.
  • Thermocouple (in an RTG, a unicouple): one such pair. Hot junction, cold junction. Material: silicon-germanium.
  • General purpose heat source, GPHS: the protective brick that holds the fuel pellets.
  • Radiator fins: carry the waste heat away so the outside stays cold.
  • Thermal power (heat, watts-thermal) versus electrical power (watts-electric). BOL / EOL: beginning of life / end of life.
  • Nuclear reactor: a machine that runs a controlled fission chain reaction. An RTG is not one.
  • Radioisotope heater unit, RHU: a small pellet used only to keep equipment warm.

🛠 How it actually works

StepThe mechanism, with numbers (Galileo's RTG, from the slide)
1 · DecayA plutonium-238 nucleus (94 protons, 144 neutrons) throws out an alpha particle and becomes uranium-234 (92 and 142). Count them: 94 − 2, 144 − 2.
2 · HeatThe alpha particle is stopped inside the fuel within a hair's width, and its energy turns into heat. One gram gives 0.57 W. Galileo's 7.5 kg: about 4,300 W of heat. The fuel sits near 1,000 °C.
3 · SeebeckHeat makes the charge carriers in a material drift from the hot end to the cold end. Two different materials drift by different amounts, so joining them at the hot end leaves a voltage between the cold ends. 572 pairs in series give about 30 V.
4 · EfficiencyHot side about 1,000 °C, cold side about 300 °C. 290 W of electricity from 4,300 W of heat: 6.7%. The other 4,000 W leaves through the fins (and helps keep the spacecraft warm).
5 · FadingPower = starting power × 0.5^(years ÷ 87.7). That is 0.8% a year. Galileo flew 14 years: 0.5^(14 ÷ 87.7) = 0.895, so 290 W → 260 W from decay alone. The thermocouples age too, which gives the slide's 250 W.
6 · Voyager470 W at launch in 1977. About 220 W today, as far as I know, so instruments are being switched off one at a time.
7 · Why this isotopeAlpha particles are stopped by a sheet of paper, so almost no shielding is needed. And 88 years is long enough for a mission but short enough to be hot. Strontium-90 (29 years) needs heavy shielding. Americium-241 (432 years) gives a fifth of the heat per gram.
8 · SafetyThe fuel is ceramic pellets clad in iridium inside graphite shells built to survive a launch accident and re-entry. Apollo 13's is lying intact on the floor of the Pacific. Plutonium-238 is not bomb material; that is plutonium-239.

▲ Where the instructor was loose

  • The name. His slides say radioisotope thermal generator. The proper name is radioisotope thermoelectric generator.
  • "They can last up to 500 years." His own slide says 100. The honest answer: the heat halves every 87.7 years and the thermocouples age as well, so useful life is decades. Voyager is at 49 years and running short.
  • "One point fifty metres long." His slide says 114 cm.
  • "Temperature is ejected from the nucleus." A particle is ejected. It is stopped inside the fuel and its energy of motion becomes heat.
  • "Really only applicable for space." The Soviet Union powered hundreds of remote lighthouses with strontium-90 units, and early heart pacemakers ran on plutonium-238.
  • "ESA is developing strontium." As far as I know, the European programme is built on americium-241. Strontium-90 was the Soviet ground choice.
  • Only for "very far from the Sun". Also for long darkness and dust: the Mars rovers, and the Apollo instruments that had to survive two-week lunar nights.
  • "New Horizons took a few poor photos of Pluto." Probably the captions. It returned sharp close-ups of Pluto in 2015.
  • Not said: the efficiency (about 6%), and that an RTG cannot be turned off. It is hot on the launch pad and has to be cooled there.
  • He was right that it is not a reactor: no chain reaction, no fission, nothing to control. And this is the "RTG is not an engine" point from Section 4.

🗣 Say it out loud

Friend: "How does Voyager still have power after fifty years with no solar panels?"
You: It carries lumps of plutonium-238, which is naturally hot because its atoms keep breaking down. Around the hot lump are hundreds of little pairs of two materials: hot on one end, cold on the other, and that difference makes a voltage. No moving parts, works in total darkness. It is not a reactor, just natural decay. It only turns about 6% of the heat into electricity and it loses a bit under 1% a year, which is why Voyager is now switching instruments off.
